# Positioning, messaging hierarchy and naming

Use when the product or business has no written positioning, when the user says "people don't get what we do" (usually a positioning problem misdiagnosed as a messaging problem), or when a name is needed.

## 1. Interrogation first (stop and wait for answers)

Ask in one message:
- **The core:** what are you building, and how does it work, in plain nouns?
- **The audience:** who feels the pain most? Describe one person and the moment they go looking.
- **The alternative:** what do they use today? A competitor, a spreadsheet, a WhatsApp group, a freelancer, nothing?
- **The vibe:** if the brand were a person, how would it speak: rebellious, clinical, warm, authoritative, witty?
- **The proof:** what do you have that is true and checkable: numbers, customers, quotes with permission, credentials, years in business?

For a local-business client (clinic, salon, tutor), the same five questions apply; the answers are shorter and the alternative is usually "the place down the road" or "doing nothing for another month". Inertia is the biggest competitor everywhere.

## 2. Positioning: Dunford's five components, in this order

Positioning answers: why choose you over every alternative, including doing nothing.

1. **Competitive alternatives.** What would they do if you did not exist? List competitors, internal hires, manual workarounds, spreadsheets, "nothing". This list decides everything after it.
2. **Unique attributes.** Provable things the alternatives cannot match. "Great service" is not unique; "same-week appointments with a named specialist" or "runs on the customer's own cloud account" is.
3. **Value for the customer.** Turn each attribute into feature -> benefit -> value. "Real-time sync" -> "no manual entry" -> "saves the CFO 10 hours a week". Only the last column belongs in a headline.
4. **Target market characteristics.** Who loves you, pays full price and refers? Describe triggers and situations, not demographics: "companies going through a merger", "clinics that just added a second doctor", "teams that outgrew Heroku".
5. **Market category.** The frame of reference the buyer uses to understand you; it sets price expectations. Choose it deliberately (a "booking tool" and a "front-desk replacement" are priced differently).

### From / To shifts to check against

| From | To |
|---|---|
| Feature-led | Value- and outcome-led |
| "Everyone" | A specific ICP and trigger |
| Obsessing over rivals | Attacking the status quo and manual workarounds |
| "We do X, Y, Z better" | "We solve the large problem of A" |
| Crowded commodity category | An ownable category or sub-category |

### Category POV narrative (use when creating or reframing a category; overkill for a local clinic)

Observation ("the world changed in X way") -> Gap ("the old way is broken or expensive") -> Ramifications ("if it stays unfixed, Z happens") -> Vision ("a new way to think about it") -> Solution ("we built [category] for this"). Coordinated attention events (a stunt, a public fight with the incumbent's assumption, a big functional release) are how a category gets noticed; plan one per launch at most.

## 3. Messaging hierarchy (four levels)

- **L1 One-liner (7-12 words):** no jargon, names the outcome. Weak: "digital-first strategy agency building brands". Better: "We build brands for B2B companies that want to charge more". For a clinic: "Physiotherapy in Vastrapur with same-week appointments".
- **L2 Elevator pitch (30 seconds, 3-4 sentences):** who it is for + what it is + why it matters + what is different. This doubles as the website headline plus subhead and the LinkedIn "about" summary.
- **L3 Proof narrative (2-3 minutes):** "a company/patient like you, their struggle, what we did, the result". If no real story exists yet, write "none yet" and collect one; do not invent a composite.
- **L4 Deep dive:** methodology, blog posts, talks, case studies, documentation.

### Voice versus tone

Voice is the consistent personality; tone adapts to context (a pricing page and an outage email share a voice, not a tone). Pick 3-4 attributes and define each as IS / IS NOT:
- Confident IS "states things plainly" / IS NOT "arrogant or dismissive of alternatives".
- Warm IS "approachable, uses the reader's words" / IS NOT "cutesy or exclamation-heavy".
- Technical IS "names the mechanism" / IS NOT "jargon as decoration".

### Eight-step sales narrative (for a pitch, a demo script or a long-form "why us" page)

Insight -> Alternatives (old way vs new way, with trade-offs) -> Perfect world (criteria an ideal solution would meet, chosen to match your strengths) -> Introduction (you, as the answer to those criteria) -> Differentiated value (2-3 pillars with the features that enable each) -> Proof -> Objections (price, implementation effort, the elephant in the room) -> The ask (one specific, low-friction next step).

## 4. Grounding positioning in the market's own words

Before writing headlines, collect verbatim language:
- Reddit threads, Hacker News comments, GitHub issues, G2 or app-store reviews, support tickets, sales-call notes; for a clinic, Google reviews of the client and of nearby competitors, and the questions the front desk hears daily.
- Prefer recent, high-engagement posts; weight complaints by how many people echo them. A theme supported by a single source is flagged as such.
- Extract: job titles, company stage ("we just raised", "bootstrapped"), tools in use ("we use X but hate..."), decision context ("I'm evaluating...", "building a business case").
- Market size stays directional ("competitive", "early", "saturated"); never produce a dollar figure from forum counts.

Turn the top 2-3 pain clusters into: a positioning statement in the market's words, an **H1 bank** (3-5 headlines, each under 8 words, each citing the quote it came from, addressing the frustrated user directly: "Export without crashing"), and a cold-email subject line per cluster.

### Competitor complaint mining (apps and SaaS)

Low-star reviews of the main alternative are a positioning map. Cluster complaints in the reviewers' words, keep clusters with at least three reviews, and look for **broken promises**: the competitor's own description makes a testable claim (feature, reliability, pricing transparency, speed) and a cluster documents the failure. Label as "Claims instant sync; users report data loss". Two rules: only attack where you have a shipped feature that fixes it, and never repeat a claim their users say is false of your product either. Superlatives, team descriptions and aspirational language are not promises. If fewer than ten low-star reviews exist, say the data is too thin rather than forcing findings.

## 5. Naming

### Principles
- The brain is lazy: a name must be "surprising, but surprisingly familiar".
- Every typed or spoken instance accrues equity, so short and pronounceable compounds over time.
- Avoid the "safe port" of literal description (ReadyMop vs Swiffer). Literal names are easy to approve and impossible to own.
- Seek the "tension zone": half the room loves it, half is uneasy. Unanimous comfort usually means forgettable.

### Diamond alignment (before generating)
Agree with the stakeholder on four points: how do we define winning; what prize or market share are we after; which perceptions or assets are missing today; the single most important message the name must carry.

### Process
1. **Identify:** map the category's naming codes (every physio clinic is "Care", "Heal", "Life"; every devtool is "-ly", "-ify", "Stack") so you can break them.
2. **Invent:** generate 20+ candidates under strict constraints per batch (one batch only liquid consonants, one only Latin suffixes, one truncation-fusion blends).
3. **Implement:** check pronunciation across the languages your users speak (for India: Hindi, Gujarati and English at minimum; make sure it is not an unfortunate word in any of them), check trademarks, check domains and social handles.

### Sound symbolism (phonosemantics)
- **Plosives P, B, T, D, K, G:** power, energy, precision (Kodak, Google, BlackBerry). Most top brand names contain one.
- **Fricatives F, V, S, Z, SH:** speed, agility, sophistication (Visa, Zoom, Vercel, Swiffer).
- **Liquids L, R:** smooth, fluid, luxury (Rolex, Lucid).
- **Nasals M, N:** comfort, warmth (Amazon, Noom).
- **Front vowels i, e:** small, fast, light, precise (Mini, Fitbit, Wii). **Back vowels o, u:** large, powerful, durable (Hulu, Volvo, Bose).
- CVCVCV structures (Dasani) are the easiest to process across languages.

### Morphological techniques
- **Morpheme blending matrix:** roots x suffixes (Ver- + -cel; "-ium" for elemental strength, "-on" for technical precision).
- **Etymological anchoring:** Latin/Greek root + modern noun (Novus, GraphBase).
- **Truncation and fusion:** Verizon (veritas + horizon), Instagram (instant + telegram).

### Diamond Test (final filter; present the top 5-7 with reasoning)
- **Distinctiveness:** does it break the category's naming codes?
- **Processing fluency:** easy to say, spell and remember; natural consonant-vowel rhythm; survives being said over the phone.
- **Suggestive relevance:** suggests a feeling or benefit without literally describing the product.
- **Energy / tension:** polarising enough to make someone pause for half a second.

### Domain and handle check
Never state availability from memory. Check DNS and RDAP (`https://rdap.org/domain/<name>.com`: 200 means registered, 404 means not found in RDAP, 403 means blocked or premium) or a registrar, and check the .in and the brand's likely social handles. Report what you checked and when.

Hand visual identity (wordmark, palette, type) to the brand-identity skill once a name is chosen.
