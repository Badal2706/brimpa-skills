# Sections, heroes and copy

How a page is composed once the direction and tokens exist: which sections a job needs, how a hero earns the first screen, what proof is allowed, and how to write the words. Percentages come from the gallery study (taste-skill, Oct 2026); structural rules from impeccable's Persuade mode and craft floor; pattern lists from the ui-ux-pro-max landing catalog.

## 1. The argument comes first

Before any layout, list the page as claims with their proof, one line per section:

```
1. It is a three-legged oak stool, made to order.          -> the stool, large
2. Each seat is cut from one board, so the grain is yours.  -> three close-ups
3. It takes six weeks, and you can follow it.               -> the stages, photographed
4. It costs 240 and is delivered by the maker.              -> price, button
```

A section with no claim is filler; delete it. The order of the argument is the order of the page, which is how you escape the stock sequence hero, logos, three features, testimonial, pricing, call to action. Short pages are fine (a quarter of studied sites are one or two screens); do not add sections to seem complete.

Also write the one sentence that makes the page belong to this client: "On this page, **[something that belongs to the subject]** becomes **[the device the direction calls for]**." Put the nearest competitor's name in it; if the sentence still works, the idea belongs to the category, not the client.

## 2. Landing-page anatomy by job

Each job lists the sections that usually carry a claim, in a defensible order. Vary the order between projects; never ship the same sequence twice in a row. "Hero" always includes the title, the supporting sentence and the primary action.

**SaaS / software product (job: start a trial or book a demo)**
- Hero with the product demonstrating itself (a working fragment, not a screenshot).
- The one problem, stated plainly, with the real before state.
- Two to four capabilities, each with its own layout (split, full-bleed interface crop, table, list), never a row of identical cards.
- How it works as three steps written as outcomes (five maximum), drawn as a process line if the direction allows.
- Proof that exists: real customer names with permission, real numbers with units and dates, or none.
- Pricing with real maths; a comparison only of things that are true (five rows maximum).
- FAQ of the questions sales actually hears.
- Close: the action again, then a designed footer.

**Local business / clinic (job: call, book, or get directions)**
- Hero: name with what it does and where ("Dental clinic in Navrangpura"), the real photograph of the place at 40% or more of the first screen if one exists, the action as the category's working form (open slots, call, WhatsApp, directions).
- Services as a list or a service-and-price table with a picture per service, not icon cards.
- The people: real portraits, same crop, name and role, credentials stated as facts.
- Proof that exists: real reviews quoted with first name and initial and where they were posted, or the aggregate from a platform you can link; otherwise none.
- Practicalities as a designed block: address, map (a real map or embed), hours computed as "open now / opens at", parking, languages, insurance.
- Close: the booking action again; footer with NAP identical to the listing (see the seo-geo-audit skill for schema and NAP consistency).

**Portfolio (job: get hired or be remembered)**
- Hero: the work itself leads from the first viewport (Experience mode); the name and one line saying what you do.
- The index: project names with year and one-line outcome, each opening to a case; the index-with-preview move suits it.
- Two or three cases in depth (problem, what was made, result with real numbers), each composed differently.
- About: one paragraph in the person's own words, a real portrait, where you are and when available.
- Close: email as a headline-sized line; footer small.

**E-commerce storefront (job: add to cart)**
- Hero: the product, large, with add-to-cart or the collection entry as the working form; price visible.
- Collection grid as the one place a repeated module is correct (an archive), one crop ratio, prices in tabular figures.
- The product's material or process shown, not described.
- Proof that exists: real reviews from the platform, verified; shipping, returns and payment facts stated plainly.
- Close: cart or collection again; footer with policies, contact, and the legal line.

**Editorial / newsletter (job: read, then subscribe)**
- Masthead and the lead piece (Read mode: the frame commits, the column stays calm).
- Recent issues or sections as a real index with dates.
- Subscribe form with only the fields required, linked to a sample issue; subscriber count only when real.
- About the writer; archive; footer.

**Event / conference (job: register)**
- Hero: what, when, where in the headline or at the second size, never only in small text; register as the primary action; countdown only if it reads the real date.
- Programme or line-up in full, as a timetable (a real `<table>` or definition list), not cards.
- Speakers with real portraits, same crop.
- Venue with a real map and travel facts; tickets with real prices and what is included; sponsors as a neutral logo row (the one logo row that is honest).
- Close: register again; footer with contact and code of conduct.

**Marketing site, multi-page (job: understand, then contact)**
- Home as the argument in brief; one page per claim that needs depth; contact as a designed page with a working form; about with real people; footer as the site map only here, set in the text face at a readable size.

Patterns from the landing catalog that remain useful as variants: minimal single column (headline, short description, three benefits maximum, one action); before/after transformation; lead magnet with the minimal form; marketplace with search as the hero action; waitlist with honest early-access benefits and no fabricated scarcity.

## 3. Hero patterns (and the ones to refuse)

What the first screen must do (Persuade mode): make the offer intelligible and desirable, expose one clear action in the category's working form, and show something only this product can prove. A stranger must be able to say in five seconds what this is and what to do. The `h1` says what it is (the name with what it does; the product with what it is for; the event with its date and place), two lines maximum; the supporting sentence sits 32-48px below it, shown with it, never docked at the foot or held back by animation; one primary action as a real button; everything readable on the first paint.

Hero types, with their share on top-scored versus weak gallery sites:

| Pattern | Top sites | Weak sites | Use when |
|---|---|---|---|
| Full-bleed media with type over it | 16% | 10% | one great photograph or clip of the real subject exists; text sits in its calm region or on a scrim |
| Giant wordmark | 14% | 0% | the name is short and the subject is visual (studios, restaurants, hotels, people); the line under it says what it is |
| Typographic poster | 13% | 7% | no honest imagery; the claim is the picture; one to five words at 8-12vw |
| Text beside media | 10% | 3% | the product or place deserves half the screen at full height; text hung from one anchor line, not centred |
| Working form as hero | - | - | the job is to search, book or configure: route and dates, open slots, a configurator, a real search box |
| Object on a held stage | - | - | one hero product (3D or image sequence) with corner-docked text and a visible step count |
| Centred statement with media below | 7% | 24% | the weak sites' signature layout; use only when the brief asks for it |

Refuse by default: the hero-metric template (big number, small label, supporting stats, gradient accent); the split hero with a badge row; a kicker or eyebrow above the headline (banned); a pill chip ("New", "Beta"); two buttons when one will do (most strong heroes have zero or one); a blinking cursor where no input exists; a fake dashboard of grey bars; a video that autoplays with sound.

Memory test: if someone left after one viewport, what would they describe an hour later? If the answer is a mood, the direction is not committed.

## 4. Section blueprints with variants

No two neighbouring sections share a layout; a shape used three times is the template showing. Pick from these shapes and recompose for each claim:

- **Split (text one side, picture the other):** text hung from the anchor line, picture full height, never both halves text; alternate sides at most once (alternating every section is a kit).
- **Full-bleed picture screen:** one image or clip at 90% of the screen with one line of type docked at an edge; the rest the page's quiet moment.
- **Statement:** one paragraph at statement size (36px or less desktop) as `<p>`, about 25 words, three lines, on its own field.
- **List:** rows of name, one-line description and a figure on one baseline; for services, menus, specs, programmes; a real `<ol>` when order matters.
- **Table:** real `<table>` with lines allowed, tabular figures, the client's own column highlighted by a field, not a glow.
- **Mosaic / strip:** a drag row of large pictures (`scroll-snap-type: x mandatory`), one ratio, for collections of up to eight; pinned sideways only on wide screens and under three screens long.
- **Held picture with passing captions:** one picture holds while three to five captions pass; a plain count ("2 of 4"); the step journey, the process, the rooms.
- **Interface fragment:** a real part of the product at true size, cropped to the point, on one flat stage colour, `data-ui` root; the strongest picture a software page has.
- **Process line:** three nodes (five maximum) connected by one drawn SVG path, each step written as an outcome; vertical on phones.
- **Bento of filled tiles:** only in a dense-tiles direction, asymmetric spans, strongest feature in the largest tile, one headline and one visual per tile, never icon-plus-text tiles of equal size.
- **Before / after:** two layers with the same crop and zoom, `clip-path: inset()`, a draggable handle with pointer events, an automatic first sweep.
- **Rolling figures:** three or four real numbers with units, rolled once; never for values that change after load.

Carry the idea into every section, pricing, FAQ and footer included: the commonest failure even among good sites is a distinctive first screen followed by stock blocks. Break the system once on purpose (one centred line, one inverted section, one picture at a different scale) so the rule is visible.

## 5. Social proof: never fake

Never invent customers, quotes from named people, press logos, user counts, ratings, sale notifications or benchmarks. Invented proof is dishonest, can breach consumer-protection law, and is a recognised tell.

- Real reviews: first name and initial, where it was posted, date; pause any shuffle on hover so people can read; aggregate rating and count only from a platform you can link to.
- Sale pings and "X people viewing": only from real orders or real data, exact amounts (round numbers look fake), never a timer loop.
- Logos: only clients who agreed; sponsors on an event page are the honest logo row.
- Numbers: real, with the unit next to the number and the period it covers ("1,240 appointments in 2025"), counted up once on first view, formatted with `toLocaleString`.
- When the brief supplies no proof, use what is true by construction: show the thing itself, large; state how it works; use facts from the brief (price, place, year, materials, what is included).
- If a testimonial or logo section is explicitly requested and nothing real exists, build it with plainly fictional sample content, no real company or person names, and say so in the report so it is replaced before launch.
- Sample details are different from proof and are expected: six rooms each with a name, a bed and a rate; every dish with a price; every speaker with a talk title. List every sample detail in the report for the client to confirm.

## 6. Pricing and comparison

- Show real monthly and annual totals and the actual saving; the "Save X%" badge must be the real maths. Default to yearly only if most revenue is annual.
- Highlight the plan that fits the intended audience with a field or an inverted card, not a glow or a gradient border; explain plan differences in plain words; free tier grey, enterprise dark is the catalog habit, not a rule.
- Three tiers is a habit, not a law; one price gets one price block, not a comparison of three plans.
- Comparison table ("us versus them"): five rows maximum, only claims that are true and verifiable, your column marked by a field, grey cross versus a check in the accent, the action under the table.
- A price roll on the billing toggle is a fine moment (see motion.md); the odometer is decoration if there is only one price.
- Tabular figures, currency symbol next to the number, the period in text ("per seat per month").

## 7. FAQ

- Real questions the client is asked, written as the visitor asks them; answers in two to four sentences, specific (prices, times, policies).
- Strip the boxes: separate items with space, a tone step or a single bottom border; a sharp plus/minus or chevron toggle; `<details>/<summary>` works without script.
- Animate the open with `grid-template-rows: 0fr` to `1fr` over 320 ms rather than measuring height.
- Six to ten questions; link out for the long policy rather than hiding it in an accordion.
- FAQPage structured data belongs to the seo-geo-audit skill once the content is final.

## 8. Navigation

- The plain form: the wordmark at one end, three to five text links (median on strong sites is 3), one action, at text size on the page ground with no bar, border or background. Link labels are single nouns.
- The nav sits on the same margins as the content (a header on one inset and the page on another is the commonest small fault).
- Fixed navigation must never sit on top of content at any scroll position; floating pill bars with blur are where this happens most, and glass is warned at two or more instances.
- On desktop the nav takes no more than about a tenth of the first screen; on a phone it is one row: wordmark and one action in view, links in a simple full-screen sheet opened by a text button ("Menu"), or moved to the footer when there are only three. A nav that eats the hero cost a whole usability point in blind tests.
- Sticky nav: add `scroll-padding-top` equal to its height so anchors and focused elements are not hidden under it.

## 9. Footer as a designed close

The page should end on a scene, not a sitemap. On strong gallery sites: the name at full width (about 18 of 114), the hero repeated in inverted colours (about 12), a closing scene of its own such as the one contact fact set as a headline (about 14), or simply one quiet line (about 22). Link-column footers appear in the panel's notes only as criticism; keep them for the multi-page marketing site's inner pages, set in the text face at a readable size.

The footer still carries the practical facts: address, hours, email, phone, legal line, image credits (one small line listing title, creator, licence and source for found pictures), on the same anchor lines as the rest of the page. A curtain footer (revealed from under the page via `position: sticky; bottom: 0`) is a legitimate move when the direction wants it.

## 10. Spacing, rhythm and the phone composition

- One pair of equal outer margins at every width: 16-32px (gallery median about 28px at 1440; never under 16px, phones included). Asymmetry lives inside the grid (an off-centre anchor line), never in the frame.
- Sections 120-240px apart on desktop (study median about 230-250px), 72-112px on a phone; a change of ground colour lets the gap be a little smaller. The gap between sections is clearly larger than any gap inside one; more than about 40% of a screen of plain ground between two sections is a hole.
- No stretch taller than about half a screen holds nothing. When text takes one half, the other half holds the section's picture, second column, list or figure, never plain ground. The first screen counts: one empty block of 28% or more of the first screen reads as a hole.
- A rest is one thing on a screen (a line, an object, a picture, a field), once per page at most; two near-empty screens fail.
- Every section opens with a heading that names it ("The rooms", "How it works", "Prices"), in the heading face at the section size, on the anchor line; 82% of text sections on studied sites open with a heading, median 57px. Small labels (caps, mono, bracketed numbers) standing in for a heading are the tell.
- Content is in place on the first paint; an entrance moves what is already there.
- Pace the scroll: alternate dense and empty, image and type, light and dark; a dense passage earns a quiet one.
- Content-driven padding: `min-height: 100vh` belongs to a section that fills the screen, not a footer or a lone heading; use `100svh`/`dvh` for mobile browser chrome.
- The phone (390px) is its own composition: one dominant thing on the first screen; re-author headline breaks; re-crop images to portrait with `object-position` per breakpoint; keep alternating widths rather than making everything full width; margins 16-20px; body 16-18px; targets at least 44px high; no sideways scroll. Three-line headlines and horizontal overflow show up here first.
- Test at 320, 375, 414, 768, 1024, 1440 (and the user's actual viewport), with real copy at every width.

## 11. Copy rules per section

General: write the whole page as plain text before markup. Plain verbs, sentence case, no filler; each written element does one job. Specific nouns and real numbers ("ships in a week", "38 seats") beat adjectives. Read every sentence and ask whether a different company could print it unchanged; if so, sharpen or cut.

- **Headline:** a title, held in one glance; about 32 characters survives every width (two-line budget: phone 390 at 44px gives about 16 characters per line). Shapes that work: the name; what it is ("Furniture for small rooms"); the claim a competitor could not sign; the instruction (verb first); the place or moment ("Lisbon, since 1984"). Avoid the mad-lib ("The AI-powered platform for modern teams"), the tricolon ("Fast. Simple. Secure."), the correction ("Not just X, it's Y"), "X, reimagined", "Welcome to", "Meet", a question nobody asked, a colon with the brand name, and one word styled differently.
- **Supporting sentence:** one sentence, at most about 22 words, concrete: what it is and who it is for; never the headline in other words.
- **Buttons:** name the action or the thing: "Book a table", "See the plans", "Download for Mac"; one to three words; the primary action keeps one name everywhere ("Publish" then the toast says "Published"); one primary per screen, a second action is a text link; at most one arrow glyph on the page. Not "Get started", "Learn more", "Submit".
- **Section headings:** two to five words naming the content ("Prices", "The team"); not "Our philosophy", "Field notes", "Quietly trusted by".
- **Body:** short paragraphs of plain statements; no marketing verbs (unlock, unleash, supercharge, elevate, empower, streamline, seamless, effortless, robust, cutting-edge, next-generation, world-class, all-in-one, blazing fast); no "theater" framing; em dashes rarely (eight or more on a page reads as a model); no explainer captions that narrate the design.
- **Steps:** written as outcomes ("Your page is live"), not tasks; no "Step 1 / Phase 02" labels duplicating numbers; three is the sweet spot, five the maximum.
- **Numbers:** unit beside the number (%, km, stars), period stated, thousands separators, locale-aware dates.
- **Errors and empty states on forms:** say what failed and how to recover, near the field, without blame; success says what happens next.
- **Furniture text to delete:** local time and coordinates strips, "Est. 2024", version stamps, "Scroll to explore", status lines with a dot ("Available for work"), strings joined by middle dots or slashes, "WORD — fragment" labels, "→" appended to every link.
- **Micro-typography:** curly quotes, real ellipsis, en dash for ranges, non-breaking space before a unit and before the last short word of a headline that would orphan.

## 12. Forms and actions without a backend

- Fields: persistent labels (placeholders are examples), correct `type` and `inputmode`, `autocomplete`, required marked consistently, inline validation on blur, specific errors referenced by `aria-describedby`, input preserved on error, the button disabled while submitting and showing its state.
- Ask only for what the action needs; a booking form for a clinic is name, phone, preferred time; everything else is friction.
- When there is no backend yet: the primary button goes to the real destination when the brief names one; otherwise `mailto:` with subject and body prefilled from the visitor's choices, or `tel:` / WhatsApp `wa.me` links for a local business; for a shop, point at `#checkout` and say in the report that it must be wired before launch. Never fake a payment form or a success state that did not happen.
- Tie any success animation to the real request's promise, never a fixed delay; on error, restore the label and say what to do.
- Forms that collect personal data from people in India need the notice and consent pattern from the dpdp-legal-compliance skill; run the security-review skill before the form goes live.
