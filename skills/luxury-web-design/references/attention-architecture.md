# Attention architecture: the buyer-journey stage model, heroes, the three tests and signature motion

## 1. The stage model and rules per stage

Visitors do not read a page; they pass through stages, and each stage uses a different part of judgement. Build each section for the stage it serves and keep the stages in order. The timings are orders of magnitude from the first-impression literature, not stopwatch rules.

**Stage 1: halo (50-500 ms).** An appeal judgement forms before anything is read and is stable (Lindgaard 2006; Tuch 2012, strong); at brief exposures it drives trust and perceived usability too (Lindgaard 2011). What drives it: low visual complexity, a recognisable skeleton, moderate colourfulness (Tuch 2012; Reinecke 2013, strong). Rules: one picture or one statement owns the first screen; element count low (wordmark, four to seven nav nouns, h1, sub-line, one button, the image); no layout shift and the display font preloaded so the first frame is the finished frame; no preloader, no cookie wall covering the hero; a ground colour sampled from the hero image so a slow image opens onto its own colour. Cheap signals: a cluttered header, blurry or stock imagery, competing buttons, badges, a promo bar.

**Stage 2: clarity (3-5 s).** The survival brain checks "am I in the right place and is this for me" before the emotional brain is allowed to want anything (practitioner model from the "three friends" video; consistent with Sillence's rapid-rejection stage). Rules: the h1 promises the buyer's outcome or names the thing exactly; the sub-line makes the value concrete (who, what, where, how much); one button names the page's job; the wordmark top-left, nav where expected, phone one tap away. Cheap signals: "Welcome to", "Learn more", jargon, five actions, a hero that sells, recruits and raises at once.

**Stage 3: emotional engagement.** Now the page may be remembered. Identity (what owning this says about the buyer), world (the coherent references the brand lives in), story (the people and the process). Picture superiority and art infusion work here; one signature moment belongs here. Rules: real people and real places; material shown close enough to feel; a world carried consistently (grading, props, language); break small patterns inside the predictable structure (an unexpected hover, an image that settles) rather than the structure itself (MAYA: most advanced yet acceptable, as framed in the source video; consistent with Tuch 2012). Cheap signals: stock or AI people, a mood with no subject, effects that exist to be admired.

**Stage 4: rational justification.** The reasons the buyer will give later for a decision already half-made: specifics, process, proof, price, guarantee. Rules: five concrete specifications; every deliverable listed; proof layered and quiet, beside the action and the price; the price rounded and plain, value sequenced before it; comparison with nothing; chunks under 75 words; features grouped in three or four chunks because working memory holds a few items (practitioner rule); for uncertain, first-time buyers, a curated choice with clear differences (Chernev 2015, moderate). Cheap signals: "premium quality", hidden pricing, star rows everywhere, "trusted by 10,000", a countdown.

**Stage 5: peak-end.** Retrospective judgement follows the peak and the end (Redelmeier and Kahneman, strong; web transfer inferential). The visit ends at the form, the booking, the bag or the thank-you, and that ending is what the buyer remembers. Rules: build and test the form first, on a phone; confirmation says what happens next and when; a short real video or a named person's note after submission is a designed peak when the brand can sustain it; the last section is a scene (the name full width, one contact fact as a headline, the hero inverted), not a sitemap. Cheap signals: a clunky form, a generic "thanks for your submission", a dead end.

## 2. The three-second first screen

The first screen must, within about three seconds of attention, deliver (as framed in the Goal-test video and consistent with the clarity stage):

1. A header that promises the thing the buyer wants (the outcome, or the thing itself named precisely).
2. A line under it that makes the value concrete.
3. One button aligned with the single goal of the page.

No "Welcome" headers. No "Learn more" (nobody knows what "more" is). If the goal is booking, the button books; if hiring, it says "Join the team"; if the goal is to let the buyer study before a boutique visit, it says "Discover the collection" or "Book an appointment" and the page is content with a conversion rate near zero because the selling happens elsewhere.

Depth still follows stakes: for a high-stakes buyer, the first screen can carry a second line or a short list (method, people, proof) without breaking the one-goal rule; what it cannot carry is a second goal.

## 3. Scan patterns and placement

- People scan rather than read. Without strong cues they default to an F-shaped pattern that misses the right side and the lower page; NN/g frames this as a design failure to prevent, not a rule to design for (Pernice 2017; moderate). Strong headings, bold key phrases and grouped blocks produce layer-cake scanning: fixations on headings, reading only when a heading is relevant.
- So: put the most informative words first in every heading; make the headings-only read tell the whole argument; bold the one phrase that matters per block; keep blocks under about 75 words.
- The Z-pattern (top-left to top-right, diagonal, bottom-left to bottom-right) is a practitioner rule of thumb for sparse screens; it is consistent with putting the wordmark top-left, the action top-right or at the end of the hero's reading path, and the headline, image and button on that path.
- About 57% of viewing time is above the fold and 74% within two screenfuls (NN/g 2018; moderate), so the proposition must be complete in the first screen and scroll cues must be real content peeking in, not an animated chevron.
- Predictive attention tools and heatmaps are useful to check where the eye lands first; the premium rule is that the hero's eye path runs headline, image, button in that order, with space (not colour) doing the pointing.
- A high-contrast primary button is not a cheap signal; an all-neutral palette where the button blends into the page is a conversion failure dressed as taste (the landing-page video's brand-book case, as described). Luxury Monochrome allows a black or white button; it still has to be the clearest thing on the screen.

## 4. Hero patterns for premium pages

`skills/web-design/references/sections.md` §3 lists hero types with gallery shares; these are the ones premium and luxury pages lean on, and how to use them:

- **The one-image stage.** One photograph or film owns the viewport; the h1 and one line sit in its calm region or on a neutral scrim; the button is plain. This is Kluge's "stage imagery" feature and the product-page rule that one image owning the viewport says "large purchase". Needs a photograph good enough to carry it; otherwise choose the next pattern.
- **Full-bleed with type over it.** The same, with the headline set large over the image; keep the image's calm region for the type, never a darkening wash over the whole frame.
- **Editorial (text beside media).** The product or place at full height on one side, text hung from one anchor line on the other; the premium service page's default when there are people to show.
- **Typographic statement.** One to five words at poster scale on a quiet ground, for brands whose claim is a word and whose imagery arrives in the second screen; a legitimate luxury hero only when the type is exceptional.
- **Object on a held stage.** One product (3D or image sequence), corner-docked text, a visible step count; the launch-page and hardware hero; the HTML headline and button readable before the scene loads.
- **Working form as hero.** For premium services whose job is booking: real slots or a short enquiry form inside the hero, styled as the brand, never a third-party widget's default look.

Refuse on premium pages: the hero-metric template, the split hero with a badge row, a pill chip ("New", "Limited"), two buttons where one will do, autoplay with sound, a carousel of offers, a promo bar above the nav.

## 5. The Goal, Memory and Care tests, with cases

**Goal test: one screen, one job.** Decide what the site is for before anything else: book, request a quote, let the buyer study the piece before visiting, recruit, raise. Cases as described in the source video: Rolex sells almost nothing online on purpose; buyers visit many times a year to study models and then buy in a boutique, so the site shows and tells and the selling happens elsewhere. A tech company found about 85% of its visitors were job candidates and rebuilt the site around careers. A dental practice lives on bookings; a chemical supplier on quote requests; a company hiring welders should make every page sell the career. A page that tries to sell, recruit and impress investors at once loses the visitor in seconds. Run it: write the job in one sentence and the one button; delete anything on the first screen that serves another job.

**Memory test: different from competitors, familiar to the buyer.** Buyers rarely buy on the first visit; the site must be remembered two weeks later, and a template with the same stock smile and the same headline as every competitor is gone by lunch. Difference buys memory (the tank-top-among-suits example); familiarity buys trust. Case as described: for a wealth-management app serving business owners aged 50+, three simple designs were tested against three award-style designs with liquid glass and 3D; of about 60 real target customers surveyed, 47 said they would never open the award-style ones (as claimed). The designers' peers would have praised them; the buyers would have left. Run it: keep the conventions (wordmark top-left, horizontal nav, phone one tap away, the booking where expected); spend difference on voice, imagery and the one signature moment; before launch, show the first screen to five real buyers and ask what they remember a day later. Evidence: low complexity plus high prototypicality is the most appealing combination (Tuch 2012, strong); older raters prefer simpler pages (Reinecke 2013, strong).

**Care test: how much did you care?** Visitors measure effort instantly. In 2026 the fastest failure is AI imagery: AI haircuts on a barbershop site, AI croissants on a bakery site, read as "we did not care enough to photograph our own shop"; a low-quality real phone photograph or a selfie video of the actual team beats a polished fake (as argued in the source video; consistent with the effort heuristic and Buell 2011). The exception: when the product is the AI, or the thing does not exist yet and a labelled 3D or render is the only honest picture. Care also shows in hundreds of small decisions the visitor cannot name but feels: the phone number findable in one look, the deposit field where expected, the size guide beside the size selector, the site fast on a weak phone, every state designed. Run it: the audit scorecard's Care row, plus a recording review of where visitors hunt and leave.

## 6. Signature motion: one cinematic moment

Mechanics, timing tokens, reduced-motion rules and the recipes are `skills/web-design/references/motion.md`; this section decides the premium motion signature.

- **One cinematic moment per page**, chosen from the subject: a picture opening from a window to full-bleed, a material revealed, a piece composing itself, a slow crossfade through three real photographs, the carried picture into the product page. It is the peak the visitor describes an hour later; spend the motion budget there and on the ending.
- **Story-paced.** Reveals arrive in reading order, once, and are settled by about 1.2 s; slowness lives in pacing (a 5-6 s crossfade, a 1.2 s opening) and never in legibility (the headline, line and button are readable on the first frame). Fluency in reading, deliberateness in pace (Reber 2004).
- **No eye candy.** Flashy animation makes visitors admire the site instead of thinking about buying; fine for a design agency's own portfolio, wrong for a service or a store (as argued across the boring-premium, landing-page and brutally-honest videos; one client's animation-heavy site was rebuilt within six months, as claimed). The one academic study found stronger animation raises how often an element is fixated, not how long attention stays. Refuse: sideways scrolling as the thesis, preloaders, kinetic type everywhere, 3D for its own sake, parallax on text, hover lift on every card, auto-rotating carousels (NN/g, moderate-weak).
- **Micro-interactions as peaks.** Press feedback, a hover that raises contrast, a form confirmation that acknowledges the person: small positive peaks that signal attention to detail (as framed in the premium-websites video; consistent with peak-end). They are quiet and immediate, never a show.
- **Autoplay video**: unproven for conversion, proven to cost LCP; if used, muted, poster first, under about 2 MB, a visible pause (WCAG 2.2 SC 2.2.2), honouring `prefers-reduced-motion`.
- **Performance is part of the signature.** LCP under 2.5 s, INP under 200 ms, CLS under 0.1 on a mid-range phone on slow 4G; every published A/B case tied better vitals to revenue (web.dev cases, moderate). A luxury page that stutters on a Rs 15,000 phone fails the care test.

## 7. Curiosity gap and picture superiority, applied

**Curiosity gap.** Curiosity responds to a perceived information gap and is strongest when the visitor knows a little, not nothing or everything (Loewenstein 1994, moderate-strong). Applied: the hero shows one specific, concrete detail and withholds the whole (a hand finishing a seam; "eight rooms, one valley, April"; the archery page's named hidden problem "target panic" followed by its definition and proof, as described in the landing-page video). "Discover more" and "Welcome" open no gap because they give nothing to be curious about. Never use it to hide the price or the job of the page; the gap is in the story, not the facts.

**Picture superiority.** Pictures are remembered better than words (Nelson, Reed and Walling 1976; replicated, strong; distinctiveness is the better explanation per the 2025 re-analysis). Applied: lead each chapter with the picture and caption it, because the caption is what the memory attaches to; one picture per screen beats six small ones; black-and-white is not a memory penalty, so a monochrome treatment is safe. Do not quote the "60,000 times" or "65% vs 10%" figures; they do not exist in the literature.

**Emotion-driven multi-imagery** (as claimed in the eight-tricks video): several real images above the fold that show the emotional outcome and the real people (the father with the baby for a reversal clinic, the grid of real therapists, the real bookkeeping staff) raise warmth and trust at once. Consistent with picture superiority and the care test. Keep the grading consistent and let one image lead so the halo stage is not crowded.
