## 1. Anti-laws of marketing as web rules

Kapferer and Bastien list 24 anti-laws (The Luxury Strategy, 2009; 2nd ed. 2012). The ones that change a web page, with the translation and the evidence that backs or qualifies each:

| # | Anti-law (paraphrased) | Web rule | Evidence note |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | Forget positioning; luxury is not comparative | No "us vs them" tables, no "best-seller" or "most popular" badges, no competitor names; the page compares with nothing and describes itself fully | Doctrine; consistent with [HAN10] on discreet signalling |
| 2 | Does the product have enough flaws? | Show the hand-finished edge, the grain, the variation; copy names the imperfection as a feature; no over-retouching | Doctrine; labour illusion [BUELL11, strong] supports showing the work |
| 3 | Do not pander to customers' wishes | One point of view; a curated range with clear differences rather than endless filters; bespoke offered as a service, not a configurator for everything | Choice-overload moderators [CHERNEV15, moderate] support curation for uncertain buyers |
| 4 | Keep non-enthusiasts out | Repel by stating price, process and who it is for; no mass-market voice | Doctrine; also the "repel the wrong customer" rule in `references/copy-and-stakes.md` |
| 5, 7 | Do not respond to rising demand; make it difficult to buy | Friction is allowed at the purchase step: appointment, waitlist, "enquire", "contact an advisor"; never at information: specs, prices, delivery, returns stay one tap away | Doctrine, constrained by Baymard's finding that luxury sites fail on search, filters, out-of-stock paths and delivery dates [BAYMARD-LUX, moderate] |
| 8 | Protect clients from non-clients | No public discount codes; client services (private appointments, engraving, repairs) named as facts; a client area if it is real | Doctrine |
| 9, 10 | Advertising is not there to sell; communicate to those you are not targeting | The homepage shows and tells to many (maison, craft, heritage pages are for everyone); the selling happens with a person or in a boutique | Doctrine; the Rolex case from the source video is the practical form |
| 11, 12 | The presumed price should seem higher than the actual; luxury sets the price | State the price plainly and rounded on every piece; no "why it costs this" paragraph beside the price; value is sequenced before it (§4) | Price as quality cue [RAO89, strong] |
| 13, 14 | Raise prices over time; raise the average price of the range | No sale section, no "was/now", no seasonal markdown wall; the ladder adds an aspirational and a halo tier (§4) | Doctrine; discount erosion §6 |
| 15 | Do not sell | Actions read "Book an appointment", "Discover", "Contact an advisor", "Request the lookbook"; no "Buy now" countdown; a plain "Add to bag" is still fine on a product page | Doctrine |
| 16 | Keep stars out of advertising | No celebrity in the hero; the maker, the advisor, named clients; peer-relatable proof | Cialdini relatability (opinion) and the premium-vs-luxury distinction: AG1-style celebrity proof is premium, not luxury |
| 17 | Cultivate closeness to the arts | Commission photography and illustration; one art-grade image per chapter | Art infusion [HAGTVEDT08, strong] |
| 18 | Do not relocate factories | Name the mill, the workshop, the city; origin and process are page content | Specificity rules in `references/imagery-and-proof.md` §7 |
| 20, 21 | Do not test; do not look for consensus | Do not A/B the brand's point of view away; test clarity, speed and findability, not whether to be quiet | Doctrine; measurement in `references/process-and-measurement.md` |
| 24 | Just sell marginally on the internet (2009) | Revised by Kapferer (2015): online is unavoidable but must reproduce rarity, service and ritual rather than efficiency; the web is "a fabulous place to sell fashion or premium products, but a dangerous one to sell luxury ones" because it removes the sensory, human and time dimensions, so the page must put them back (sensory imagery, named people, paced reveals) | Doctrine; Okonkwo 2009 argues the same transfer [OKONKWO09] |

Premium vs luxury: a premium brand (AG1, Apple, a top clinic) competes on being the best and may stack certification, review counts and expert endorsement loudly; a luxury brand competes on being the only one and keeps proof quiet. The stakes test in SKILL.md decides which register the page is in; most client work sits at premium.

## 2. What luxury homepages measurably share (Kluge 2013)

Kluge, Konigsfeld, Fassnacht and Mitschke (2013) content-analysed 81 luxury homepages with expert interviews, then ran a randomised lab experiment. Four features distinguished luxury from conventional homepages:

1. Darker or deep-neutral backgrounds (not necessarily black; deep, warm or stone neutrals count).
2. Larger or full-screen stage imagery: one picture or film owns the first screen.
3. A horizontal navigation bar (four to seven items; often behind a menu on mobile).
4. Far fewer on-page elements.

The luxury-styled homepage raised perceived conspicuousness and uniqueness, not a generic "luxury" score, and the authors asked for field tests on conversion that have not appeared. Treat the four features as the starting constraints for the luxury tier's direction shortlist (Luxury Monochrome, Dark Cinematic, Quiet Editorial, 3D Showcase in web-design's catalog) and remember that "darker" is a tendency, not a rule: a white stage with one picture also satisfies features 2-4.

Current practice (a 2026 survey of Hermes, Loro Piana, Brunello Cucinelli, Cartier, Van Cleef, Rolex, Aesop and Bang & Olufsen homepages, not a study) adds: serif or high-contrast display paired with a quiet sans; no visible discounts, timers, star ratings or "bestseller" badges; actions phrased as "Discover", "Book an appointment", "Contact an advisor"; editorial pages for maison, savoir-faire and heritage; product pages leading with materials, dimensions, origin and craft, price stated plainly, and services (complimentary delivery, gift wrapping, engraving, returns) listed as facts.

## 3. White space, logo prominence and the effort heuristic

**White space** reads as elegance, prestige and refined taste because a century of advertising taught readers that only those who could afford to waste space did so; it works as costly signalling and is tied to perceived quality and price (Pracejus, Olsen and O'Guinn 2006; follow-ups 2012-2013; moderate-strong). Crowding reads as discount. On a page: one image per screen, sections 160-240px apart, margins wide where the direction allows, a single statement given a whole field. Caveat: white space without bespoke substance reads as laziness (see effort heuristic); fill the page with few, finished things, not with nothing.

**Logo prominence.** Han, Nunes and Dreze (2010) found that among 465 Gucci and Louis Vuitton bags, louder branding correlated with lower price, and that wealthy buyers with low need for status (patricians) pay more for discreet marks that only peers recognise; loud marks serve the status-anxious and are what counterfeiters copy (strong). On a page: a small wordmark, one placement in the nav and one in the footer, no logo watermark on images, no badge clutter, no logo wall unless the logos are the proof. Quiet luxury's "we do not need to convince you" is the same signal in a sentence.

**Effort heuristic.** People trust what they sense took effort, and showing the work raises perceived value (Buell and Norton 2011, strong). The difference between curation and a lazy template is visible effort: bespoke photography instead of stock, a nuanced palette (slate and off-white with one confident accent instead of flat black and white), refined interactions, specific copy, consistent grading. Every reduction must be matched by something made.

## 4. Pricing

Words for the pricing page are launch-marketing's; this section decides how price is presented on a premium page.

**Anchoring.** The first number reframes every later one. Honest anchors: a higher tier that exists, retail's good/better/best, a deliberately expensive piece beside the one you want sold (a $900 candle beside the $200 one, as described in the pricing video), a reference price you can defend. Dishonest anchors (a crossed-out price nobody paid) are a consumer-protection problem.

**Price as a quality signal.** Buyers lack perfect information (two "100% cashmere" labels can be very different goods) and read price as a proxy; a meta-analysis of 36 studies confirms the heuristic (Rao and Monroe 1989, strong), with thin evidence at the top end. Underpricing a superior product lowers expectations and the chance of being tried, by buyers and by stockists; the video's example is an $80 shirt that boutiques pass over beside $400-500 shirts of lower quality (as claimed). Underpricing is a brand problem, not only a margin problem.

**Rounded vs charm endings.** Nine-endings drive volume in mass and value markets; rounded numbers read as prestige and candour ("we are not trying to trick you") in premium and luxury (as claimed in the pricing video; consistent with launch-marketing's rule and with the fluency account). Lost price points: buyers settle at 49, 59 and 99 and skip 69 and 79; move to the adjacent familiar point. Premium pages use rounded numbers consistently: Rs 18,000, $400, EUR 1,200.

**The four-tier ladder** (as described in the pricing video; the structure, not the figures, is the lesson):

| Tier | Job | Aesop | Apple | Loro Piana | Glossier | Agency |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Entry | low-risk gateway into the world; must be excellent | hand soap about $27 | iPhone SE, AirPods | scarves and beanies $400-600 | brow product $18 | $99 audit |
| Core | the repeat revenue | skincare $60-100 | iPhone, MacBook Air | sweaters and outerwear $1,500-3,000 | skincare $35 | $1,000 a month retainer |
| Aspirational | the status anchor that makes the core feel reasonable | gift kits and fragrance about $200 | MacBook Pro, Pro Display | the $12,000 jacket | fragrance $75 | the flagship project |
| Halo | the ceiling; may barely sell or not be for sale | - | Vision Pro | - | - | a one-of-one engagement |

Two mistakes: building too many tiers before the core is proven; building only a core, with no gateway to acquire buyers and no aspirational piece to anchor the price. Halo products (a $40,000 car in a streetwear shoot, a one-of-one $10,000 bike) exist to define the ceiling; Oreo's collaborations sold little themselves and lifted the main line (as claimed).

**Show the price or "from".** Hidden pricing on high-ticket reads as expensive and wastes calls; a published floor ("from Rs 45,000", "fees from 0.6% a year", "consultations from $300") filters buyers who were never going to pay and relaxes the ones who can (practitioner rule, consistent across the videos). State what the floor includes. Bespoke work states the process and the starting point.

**Value sequencing on a product page.** Budget order: price, discount, badges, urgency, then the product. Premium order: image, name, price, story, specifics, fit, proof; the price arrives early and plainly and is then justified six or seven times before the button (as described in the product-page video; see `references/imagery-and-proof.md` §6).

## 5. Real scarcity vs fake urgency

**Real scarcity raises value.** Lynn's 1991 quantitative review (strong) and Cialdini's synthesis: a thing that can be possessed, is useful and is transferable gains value when scarce. Legitimate forms: a numbered run, a drop that sells out and stays sold out, a seasonal colour that never returns, eight rooms, a waitlist, by appointment, a cohort with a start date. Say it once, as a fact, in the brand's voice.

**Fake urgency lowers trust and carries legal risk.** Tuncer et al. (2023/2024; N=202; three identical shops with no cues, limited-time cues, limited-quantity cues) found scarcity cues were read as a lack of benevolence, limited-time cues produced frustration, and the cue-free shop scored higher on hedonic and pragmatic UX (moderate-strong). Related: limited-time notices raised perceived scarcity without raising purchase intention (Song, Choi and Moon 2022); supply-scarcity appeals helped only buyers high in need for uniqueness and reduced intention for others. Mathur et al. (2019) found 157 deceptive countdown timers across 140 of about 11,000 shopping sites and a retailer using a random-number generator for "just sold" counts. The FTC's 2022 report names fake countdown timers and false low-stock messages as deceptive. In India, the 2023 guidelines on dark patterns under the Consumer Protection Act name false urgency similarly; check current text before advising.

Practitioner confirmation (as claimed in the product-page video): a $140 jacket with a five-minute countdown makes the buyer conclude it is not worth the money; the test is to turn timers off on evergreen products for two weeks and watch average order value, not only conversion.

Rules: no countdown on anything evergreen; a countdown only reads a real date and only for an event; stock counts only from the inventory system and only when low stock is true; no "X people viewing"; no spin-to-win; no exit-intent discount popup.

## 6. Discount erosion

Constant 40-60% discounts train the list to wait and reprice the product in the buyer's head; a premium brand that blasts discounts monthly damages identity for a short-term sale (as argued in the premium-branding video; consistent with launch-marketing's no-perpetual-discounts rule). Permitted: a launch offer with a real end date, a client price for existing clients communicated privately, a clearly bounded seasonal sale said once. Never on the homepage of a luxury-tier brand. The "marketing vector" idea from the same video is useful: write down the tactics the brand will never use (discount blasts, countdowns, influencer giveaways) and the latitude it keeps for testing, so every campaign and page stays inside it.

## 7. Quiet proof

The same information in two registers: loud reads as marketing and is discounted by the reader; quiet reads as fact.

- Style every review or rating widget in the brand's type and palette; the default teal badge of a review app is a cheap signal (as claimed; a care-test failure).
- Fewer, better: three detailed paragraphs about how a jacket held up through a winter beat a hundred "love it" reviews; curate highlights rather than dumping 1,500.
- Editorial proof: press, stockists, collaborations, physical stores, named advisors, client letters with permission.
- For true luxury, no star rows on the product; where reviews exist (premium tier), brand-coloured ratings and curated highlights, and purchase likelihood peaks at 4.0-4.7 stars, not 5.0 [SPIEGEL17].
- Proof sits where hesitation peaks: beside the action and beside the price (practitioner rule; no controlled study).
- Nothing fabricated, ever; the honesty floor in `skills/web-design/references/sections.md` §5 applies unchanged.

## 8. Calibrating restraint to buyer stakes

Most sites carry two or three times the words, sections and effects they need; cut by half, then by half again, and what survives looks deliberate (as claimed in the boring-premium video). Then calibrate: how much to strip depends on how much the buyer stands to lose. A sandwich or a haircut wants a minimal page and an instant action. A wealth-consulting firm managing life savings cannot build trust on a three-word page; it needs thorough sections on method, services and the founder so the buyer sees a real human (the Attune case, as described). The expensive-websites video gives the mirror case: a client wanted a minimal hero; audience data said buyers needed more upfront; doubling the above-the-fold content nearly doubled conversion (as claimed). The stakes set the amount of content; the doctrine sets its form: few, finished, specific things with room around them.
