# Copy and stakes: what the words must do on a premium page

The words themselves (headlines, positioning, de-slop, pricing-page copy) are written with `launch-marketing`; this file sets the copy rules a premium or luxury page imposes on them: how deep, how specific, which desire, and what to cut. ## 1. Design earns the look, words win the client

The sites that quietly lose clients often already look polished: high-end design sets high expectations, and vague messaging creates the disconnect that makes the visitor leave (as argued in the coaches video, from 150+ builds). Good design cannot carry bad copy. Design gets the visitor to stay for a second; the words get them to stay for thirty and decide. The visitor is the hero and the brand is the guide: never open with the brand's credentials ("Certified coach with fifteen years' experience"); open with the buyer's aspiration, then how the brand gets them there, then one action.

## 2. Life Force 8 and headline patterns

Sell the core desire, not the surface outcome. Whitman's eight biologically programmed desires, with the headline pattern each produces (patterns adapted from the landing-page psychology video's examples; the list itself is Whitman's, opinion-grade but a useful inventory):

| Desire | Pattern | Example shape |
|---|---|---|
| Care and protection of loved ones | "Protect <who> from <what>" | a pool fence page leading with the children, not the fence |
| Freedom from fear, pain, danger | "Before it is too late" stated plainly, once | a security audit that names the loss, not the feature |
| Social approval | "<the result> others will notice" | a lawn, a garden, a facade |
| Superiority and status | "What <the discerning> choose" | a quiet "for the few who" without saying the word luxury |
| Attractiveness and companionship | the compliment as testimony | a clinic page where a client reports what people said |
| Comfortable living | the restful scene as headline | a bathroom, a hotel room, a chair |
| Survival and long life | the capability kept | a training or health page leading with what the buyer keeps doing |
| Enjoyment of food and drink | the sensory specific | a dish, a vintage, named |

For luxury, the status and approval desires are the loudest in reality and the quietest on the page; let imagery and specifics carry them and let the copy name the craft. Never stack several desires in one headline; one per page, one per section.

## 3. Loss aversion and the labour illusion in copy

**Loss aversion.** Losses weigh more than equivalent gains (Kahneman and Tversky; strong in the lab, used here as a copy frame). A shipping-audit page that opened with "you are losing $30,000 to $50,000 on every $1 million shipped" outperformed a gains framing (as claimed). Use it once, with a real, defensible number and the method behind it; never as a scare, never with a timer.

**Labour illusion in copy.** Showing the work raises perceived value (Buell and Norton 2011, strong): list every deliverable and every step of a high-price service, name the hours and the people, show the process as outcomes. A $5,000 engagement listed as "strategy and design" looks expensive; the same engagement listed as fourteen named deliverables across six weeks looks like what it costs. On product pages, the five construction specs and the process story do the same job (`references/imagery-and-proof.md` §7).

## 4. Awareness levels and personas (intent, action, awareness)

**Awareness** (Schwartz, Breakthrough Advertising; opinion-grade framework used across the industry): most aware (knows the brand, on the cusp), product aware, solution aware (knows solutions exist, has not chosen), problem aware (feels the problem, knows no solution), unaware. Meet the visitor where they are: a most-aware page can lead with the offer; a problem-aware page must first name the problem in the buyer's words (the archery page named "target panic", defined it, then proved it with recognisable names, as described), and an unaware audience is the hardest and most expensive to convert. Copy cannot create desire; it can only focus desire that exists onto the product (Schwartz), so sell what the market already wants (the operations-coaching offer that "sold like a gym membership" against the sales-and-clients offer that "opened the doors", as described).

**Personas from data.** Ditch the whiteboard persona; paid surveys over-sample one kind of customer. Derive personas from orders, the email list, CRM notes, call recordings and reviews; tools now cluster a customer base into segments with share and lifetime value (as described in the pricing video; tool names omitted here because they change). Minimum useful persona: who, what they buy, how often, what they say the problem is, how aware they arrive, where they come from.

**The triad per persona** (as framed in the psychology-improves-websites video): **intent** (what they came to accomplish), **action** (the exact next step, plus how the brand keeps in touch if they leave), **awareness** (what they already know, which dictates the content and the next step).

## 5. Specificity over vagueness; clear over clever

- "We have Kansas City covered" told the visitor only the city; "luxury wedding transportation, limousines and party buses" with the city as a sub-line let the firm raise rates by over 30% (as claimed). Specific nouns, real numbers, the actual service.
- "Let's build that life you have been dreaming about" could belong to any business; the rewrite named who she helps and what problem she solves (as described). A visitor does not want to decode the brand; they want to know if it is for them.
- The three-question test before any homepage copy: who is the ideal client, what is their most pressing problem, how do you solve it. The voiceover artist's page named marketing professionals anxious about generic voices and same-week turnarounds, then stacked credits and a project-management background (as described). launch-marketing runs this test; the premium page requires that all three answers are visible in the first two screens.
- Specificity is also the premium register: it implies competence and effort; vagueness implies a template.

## 6. Readability and 75-word blocks

- Nobody reads; they scan headings, bold phrases and pictures. Blocks over about 75 words are skipped (as claimed; consistent with NN/g scanning evidence). Keep every block under it, bold one phrase, and run the headings-only read: if the page does not make sense from the headings alone, it is not scannable yet.
- Short words, short sentences, short paragraphs (Ogilvy). Aim for a grade 5-9 reading level with a readability checker; an IT page that opened with "cybersecurity posture" converted under 1% until it was rewritten at a fifth-grade level (as claimed). Persuasive and simple is hard; keep the essence and cut the syntax.
- White space slows reading and raises comprehension and enjoyment; crammed sales pages read faster and are understood less (as argued, citing studies not named in the video; consistent with fluency and complexity evidence). Give each idea its own section; let off-white or light-grey fields act as chapters.
- AI tells to strip (from the coaches video and launch-marketing's de-slop list): em-dash saturation, everything in threes, random "actually", abstract nouns given agency ("this approach unlocks potential"), buzzwords. The surviving copy after a radical cut must be sharp human sales copy, or the reduction reads as empty.

## 7. Cut by half twice, then calibrate to stakes

1. Cut the page's words, sections and effects by half; it still makes sense. Cut by half again; what survives looks deliberate (as claimed in the boring-premium and brutally-honest videos: most pages carry two to three times what they need; cut copy by about 75%).
2. Then calibrate to the buyer's stakes. Low stakes (a sandwich, a haircut): keep it minimal. High stakes (savings, surgery, a year-long contract, a once-in-a-life purchase): add back, above the fold, the method, the people, the process and the proof, because minimalism at high stakes reads as evasion. The wealth-consulting case needed thorough sections on methodology, services and the founder; the expensive-websites case doubled above-the-fold content against the client's wish and nearly doubled conversion (both as claimed).
3. Depth is measured in specifics and proof, not in sections. A deep page can still have five sections if each is exact.
4. Lead-generation pages are the exception that proves the rule: for an opt-in with a low-stakes ask, removing everything below the fold lifted conversion from 2.32% to 3.51% in one A/B test (as claimed, N about 1,300 per arm). Application pages pre-sell and filter before a call; direct-sale pages warm up and route to the product page.

## 8. Kill corporate ego; the About page flip

- "Founded in 1986", board messages, milestone timelines and a chairman's letter flatter the firm and answer no buyer question. Ask of every section: is this here for the buyer or for us? Keep heritage only as what it bought the buyer ("forty years of flow monitoring means the part you need is in stock and the engineer who answers has fitted one").
- The About page is usually the second most visited and usually wasted on a resume. Flip it: open with the customer's transformation, give hard metrics, show real faces, and let the founder's story serve the buyer's confidence (as argued in the brutally-honest video).
- Sell the result, not the feature; the feature appears as the specific that proves the result.

## 9. Repel the wrong customers

A premium page qualifies as hard as it attracts: state the price or the floor, name who it is for and who it is not for, describe the process and its demands, and let the wrong buyer leave early. This is anti-law 4 (keep non-enthusiasts out) in plain clothes and the practitioner rule to "actively repel the wrong customers" (brutally-honest video). launch-marketing's positioning step already checks that the positioning repels as deliberately as it attracts; the premium page makes that visible in the first two screens. Look like a commodity and you are priced like one: a plastic template competes on price; a distinct human point of view does not.

## 10. The personalisation triad and segmented pages

Where personas differ in intent or awareness, one page that hedges converts worse than two that commit. Build a landing page per major persona (the wellness professional buying for work vs the biohacker buying for self, as described), duplicate the structure and swap copy, imagery and proof; segment emails and messages by persona and lifetime value. The homepage stays the brand's argument; the segmented pages are where the ads land. For a clinic: the patient page and the referring-doctor page; for a firm: the founder-selling page and the family-office page.

## 11. Copy checklist for the premium page

- The h1 names the buyer's outcome or the thing exactly; no credentials, no "Welcome", no cleverness that needs decoding.
- The three answers (who, what problem, how) are visible in the first two screens.
- One desire per page; status and approval carried by imagery and specifics, not adjectives.
- Five construction or service specifics on every hero product or service page.
- Every deliverable and step listed for high-price services.
- Price or "from" stated, rounded, with what it includes.
- No block over about 75 words; headings alone tell the story; grade 5-9 readability unless the audience reads jargon for a living.
- Cut by half twice; depth restored only where stakes demand it.
- No corporate ego sections; About page flipped to the customer.
- The wrong customer can tell within two screens that this is not for them.
- AI tells and buzzwords stripped (launch-marketing's list); no fabricated numbers; every statistic sourced or removed.
