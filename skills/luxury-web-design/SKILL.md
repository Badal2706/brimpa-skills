# Luxury web design: strategy and psychology for premium-to-luxury sites

A premium site is not a normal site with more white space. It is a different argument: the visitor must judge the brand as expensive before reading a word, then find every reason to believe it, then remember it two weeks later when they are finally ready to call. `web-design` decides how a page looks and builds it; this skill decides what a high-budget page must do to be worth its price, and what it must refuse. The research behind every rule is graded in `references/evidence.md`; statistics that come only from a practitioner video are marked "as claimed in the source video" and never treated as facts.

Scope: public pages for brands that charge the top of their category (premium services, luxury goods, high-ticket D2C, boutique hospitality, clinics with a premium position, agencies and product launches that need to be noticed). Also a premium audit of an existing page. App screens stay with `ui-ux`; naming, taglines and launch copy stay with `launch-marketing`; logo, palette and voice gaps go to `brand-identity`.

## When to use this vs web-design: the stakes test

Answer five questions for the brief. Each answer falls in one column; the column with most answers is the tier.

| Question | Normal (web-design alone) | Premium (load both) | Luxury (load both; this skill constrains direction and motion too) |
|---|---|---|---|
| Price point: what does a typical purchase cost? | lunch, a haircut, a monthly app | top third of its category; a few hundred to a few thousand dollars, or Rs 20,000 to a few lakh | top decile or a price the buyer will not say out loud; bespoke, numbered, by appointment |
| Buyer risk: what does the buyer lose if wrong? | an hour and a small sum | a season, a project, their face in front of a client | savings, health, reputation, a once-in-a-life purchase |
| Brand ambition: compete how? | on price and availability | be the best in the category | be the only one; "we do not need to shout" |
| Budget tier (scope framing from the price-tier video; see `references/process-and-measurement.md`) | template execution | custom design, strategy, CMS, motion | strategy weeks, research, copywriting, photography, QA layers, accountability |
| Attention goal | visitors already know what this is | stand out in a crowded category | a launch that must be remembered and talked about |

Normal: run `web-design` only; its house rules already carry the cheap always-on basics (one action, real photos, proof beside the button). Premium: run both; this skill sets proof, imagery, pricing presentation and copy depth. Luxury: run both; this skill additionally restricts the direction shortlist, applies the anti-laws and sets the motion signature. Say the tier out loud in the first line of the brief, because every later choice depends on it.

Scoring notes: ties go to the higher tier when buyer risk is in the luxury column (health, savings, reputation), and to the lower tier when the budget is in the normal column, because a luxury strategy cannot be executed on a template budget and saying so is the first consultative act. A brand can be premium in price and loud in register (a streetwear drop, a launch for a young audience); the register is decided at step 4, not by the tier. When the user's words are "premium" but the answers are all in the normal column, run web-design and tell them why.

## How to run this

Steps 1-5 are strategy and take longer than the design; on a premium engagement they are what the client is paying for. Steps 6-11 set the rules the build must follow. Step 12 hands over.

1. **Gate and goal.** Run the stakes test. Then run the Goal test: one screen, one job. Decide what the site exists to do (book a consultation, request a quote, let a buyer study the piece before visiting the boutique, recruit, raise). Rolex's site has almost no checkout by design: buyers study models for a year and buy in a boutique (as described in the source video). A page that sells, recruits and impresses investors at once fails all three. Write the job in one sentence and the one button that serves it.
2. **Discovery and research.** The unseen layers of an expensive site, done before any design tool opens: discovery calls, a competitor audit (five to eight direct competitors and two aspirational brands outside the category), the market gap, sitemap, content hierarchy, wireframes. Expect to push back on the brief with data: a client who asks for a three-word hero when their buyers are high-stakes gets shown the audience evidence, not the hero. Artefact list and timings in `references/process-and-measurement.md` §1-2.
3. **Persona and awareness mapping.** For each buyer group write intent (what they came to do), action (the exact next step) and awareness (unaware, problem-aware, solution-aware, product-aware, most-aware). Derive personas from real buyer data (orders, CRM, call notes, reviews), not a whiteboard; a paid survey over-samples one kind of customer. Where personas differ sharply (buying for work vs for self; patient vs referring doctor), plan separate landing pages rather than one page that hedges. Detail in `references/copy-and-stakes.md` §4.
4. **Positioning.** Stack three triggers in order: identity signalling (what owning this says about the buyer), world-building (the coherent world the brand borrows and builds: materials, rituals, references from outside the category), counterposition (what everyone on autopilot does, done the other way). Decide quiet or loud by the buyer's stakes and status needs: the wealthiest, least status-anxious buyers pay more for discreet branding (Han, Nunes and Dreze 2010, strong), so a small wordmark and few logos is a decision, not timidity; a launch for a young audience can be loud and still be premium. Pillars of the premium story: exclusivity (a narrative, not a stock counter), identification (the product as mirror), storytelling (the world the buyer steps into). Naming, taglines and messaging hierarchy go to `launch-marketing`; this step only fixes the position the page must hold.
5. **Attention architecture.** Map every section to a stage of the buyer journey and give it that stage's job: 50-500 ms halo (one finished-looking first viewport, no layout shift), 3-5 s clarity (a header that promises what the buyer wants, one concrete sub-line, one button), emotional engagement (the world, the material, the people), rational justification (specifics, proof, process, price), peak-end (one signature moment and a designed ending: the confirmation, the thank-you, what happens next). Reorder sections so the stages run in order; delete sections that serve no stage. Full stage model, scan patterns and hero patterns in `references/attention-architecture.md`.
6. **Imagery standard.** Decide what will be photographed, by whom, and when, before the layout depends on it. Premium imagery is bespoke, real, consistently graded, and lets one image own the viewport. The five product shots for sensory transfer (editorial in context, macro texture, movement, hardware detail, clean silhouette), real people in real places, and never AI imagery for product, people, food or places: visitors read it as "they did not care enough to photograph their own shop". Art and painterly photography beside a product raise its perceived luxury (Hagtvedt and Patrick 2008, strong). Standard and shot lists in `references/imagery-and-proof.md` §1-3.
7. **Proof architecture.** Decide which layers of proof exist and where each sits: expert or press, certification, review count or client letters, a study or audited numbers, a guarantee. Peer-relatable proof beats celebrity; a screenshot of the source beats a retyped quote; style every review widget in the brand's own type and colour; feature a few detailed reviews over a hundred two-word ones. For true luxury, star ratings on the product are off-register: use editorial press, named advisors, stockists and client letters. Never fabricate anything. Rules in `references/imagery-and-proof.md` §4-6.
8. **Copy depth and pricing presentation.** Cut copy by half twice, then calibrate to stakes: a high-stakes buyer needs method, people, process and proof above the fold or the restraint reads as evasive. Sequence value before price (image, name, price, story, specifics, fit, proof), state five concrete specifications, write to the core desire, list every deliverable (labour illusion), kill corporate ego. Show the price or "from"; use rounded prices; no discounts, no timers, no popups; anchor with a real higher tier, not a fake "was" price. Rules in `references/copy-and-stakes.md` and `references/luxury-doctrine.md` §4.
9. **Motion signature.** One cinematic moment per page, paced like a story, settled fast, nothing that exists to be admired. Decide the moment from the subject (a picture opening to full-bleed, a material revealed, a piece composing itself) and write it into the brief; everything else is feedback and quiet entrances. Flashy motion makes visitors admire the site instead of buying, and the one premium-neutral study of attention-grabbing elements found animation raises how often an element is looked at, not how long. Rules in `references/attention-architecture.md` §6; mechanics in `skills/web-design/references/motion.md`.
10. **Memory and Care tests.** Memory: different from competitors, familiar to buyers. Put difference into voice, imagery and the signature moment; keep the skeleton conventional (wordmark top-left, horizontal nav, phone one tap away). The wealth-management case: of 60 target customers aged 50+, 47 said they would never open the award-style designs (as claimed in the source video); low complexity plus a recognisable skeleton is also what the first-impression studies find most appealing (Tuch et al. 2012, strong). Care: real photographs, every small decision done right, the phone number and the booking field findable in one look. Cases in `references/attention-architecture.md` §5.
11. **Measurement plan.** Write how success will be judged before launch: category benchmarks over opinion, the five-second stranger test, the headings-only skim, heatmaps and recordings, hook rate, hold rate, click-through and cohort retention alongside conversion, a VisAWI short-form on the prototype, and the two-week memory test. Post-launch loop in `references/process-and-measurement.md` §4-5.
12. **Hand off to the build.** With `web-design` loaded: pass the Premium Strategy Brief; web-design runs its direction draw inside the constraints below, writes tokens, builds and runs its pre-flight; this skill re-checks the built page with the Premium Audit scorecard. Alone: produce the brief, the section plan and a build spec a developer can follow without this skill (see "Alone mode").

## Luxury doctrine in brief

Kapferer and Bastien's anti-laws of marketing are the dominant professional doctrine for luxury (opinion-grade, but consistent with the signalling studies). The ones that matter online, translated (full table with sources in `references/luxury-doctrine.md` §1):

| Anti-law (paraphrased) | On the page |
|---|---|
| Luxury is not comparative | No comparison tables, no "best-seller" or "most popular" badges; the page compares with nothing |
| A signature imperfection is a source of emotion | Show the hand, the grain, the atelier; do not retouch the craft out |
| Do not pander to the customer | One point of view; curated ranges, not endless filters; bespoke is a service, not a configurator for everything |
| Keep non-enthusiasts out; make it (slightly) difficult to buy | Friction at the purchase step is allowed (appointment, waitlist, "enquire", "contact an advisor"); never at information |
| Protect clients from non-clients | No public discount codes, no mass-market voice; private client services named as facts |
| Advertising is not there to sell; communicate to those you are not targeting | The site shows and tells to many; the selling happens with a person; maison, savoir-faire and heritage pages are for everyone |
| Luxury sets the price; the presumed price should seem higher than the actual | State the price plainly and rounded; no anchoring games, no "was/now", no justification paragraph beside the price (value was sequenced before it) |
| Raise prices over time; do not sell | No sale section, no timers, no popups; actions read "Book an appointment", "Discover", not "Buy now" |
| Keep stars out | No celebrity hero; the maker, the advisor and named clients instead |
| Cultivate closeness to the arts | Commission photography and illustration; art infusion raises perceived luxury of what sits beside it |
| Sell marginally on the internet (2009) | Revised by Kapferer in 2015: online is unavoidable but must reproduce rarity, service and ritual, not efficiency |

What the evidence adds: luxury homepages measurably share darker or deep-neutral grounds, a full-screen stage image, a horizontal nav and far fewer elements, and that style raised perceived uniqueness and conspicuousness in a lab test (Kluge et al. 2013, moderate). White space is a decoded prestige signal with an advertising history; crowding reads as discount (Pracejus et al. 2006, moderate-strong). Price is a quality cue (Rao and Monroe 1989, strong). Rounded prices read as prestige and honesty, charm prices as volume (as claimed in the pricing video; consistent with launch-marketing's pricing rules). Genuine scarcity raises value (Lynn 1991, strong); manufactured urgency lowers perceived benevolence and is treated as deceptive by regulators (Tuncer et al. 2023; Mathur et al. 2019; FTC 2022). Showing the work raises perceived value even when it costs time (Buell and Norton 2011, strong): the effort heuristic is why bespoke photography, nuanced palettes and refined interactions read as curation while a bare template reads as laziness. Calibrate restraint to the buyer's stakes: minimalism reads as evasive when the buyer's savings or health are on the line.

## Journey stage rules (short form)

Each section serves one stage; the stages run in page order. Full rules in `references/attention-architecture.md` §1.

- **Halo (50-500 ms).** The first viewport looks finished before anything is read: one picture or statement, few elements, no layout shift, the display font already loaded. Low visual complexity and a recognisable skeleton are the two measured drivers of appeal (Tuch et al. 2012; Reinecke et al. 2013, strong); older and more educated audiences prefer simpler, less colourful pages.
- **Clarity (3-5 s).** The h1 names the buyer's outcome or the thing itself, the sub-line makes it concrete, one button names the job. Nothing animates the words into view; they are there on the first frame.
- **Emotion.** The world arrives: real people, real place, material close enough to feel, one art-grade image; identity and story before features; the signature moment usually lives here.
- **Justification.** Specifics, process, proof and price, in that order; chunks under 75 words; a buyer skimming only headings still gets the argument; comparison with nothing.
- **Peak-end.** The ending is designed: the form or booking tested first on a phone, a confirmation that says what happens next and when; the last section is a scene, not a sitemap.

## Attention hooks without cheapness

- **Curiosity gap.** Show one specific, concrete detail (a hand finishing a seam, one number from the atelier) and withhold the whole; curiosity needs partial knowledge, so "Discover more" opens no gap (Loewenstein 1994, moderate-strong).
- **Picture superiority.** Lead with the image and caption it; pictures are remembered better than words (strong), but the "60,000 times faster" and "65% vs 10%" figures are not from any study.
- **Art infusion.** One painterly photograph or commissioned illustration beside the product; dilutes with repetition.
- **Labour illusion.** Make the work visible: process footage, "composing your piece" states, every step of a bespoke service listed.
- **One signature cinematic moment.** The peak the visitor describes an hour later; spend the motion budget there and on the ending (peak-end, strong principle, inferential transfer to web).
- **Distinctiveness done right.** Von Restorff is difference relative to a similar context, not loudness: one serif word in a sans headline, one object in colour on a monochrome spread, one highlighted tier; three accents cancel it.
- **Different yet familiar.** Unusual surface, conventional skeleton; the 47-of-60 survey (as claimed) and Tuch 2012 point the same way.
- **The three-second first screen.** Header that promises the buyer's outcome, sub-line that makes it concrete, one button for the page's job; no "Welcome", no "Learn more".
- **Placement.** The F-pattern is the default when headings are weak and a design failure, not a rule; strong headings and bold phrases produce layer-cake scanning, so put the most informative words first and let the hero's eye path run headline, image, action.
- **Emotion-driven multi-imagery.** Several real images of the emotional outcome and the real people above the fold (the father with the baby for the clinic, the real staff for the firm), as claimed in the source video; consistent with the picture-superiority and care evidence.

## Pricing presentation (short form)

Full reasoning, the ladder table and the two mistakes are in `references/luxury-doctrine.md` §4; the pricing-page words are launch-marketing's.

1. Show the price, or "from" with what it includes. Hidden pricing on high-ticket reads as expensive and wastes calls; a published floor filters buyers who were never going to pay it.
2. Round the numbers (Rs 18,000, $400), never charm endings; move off lost points like 69 and 79 to the adjacent familiar one.
3. Sequence value before the price on a product page: image, name, price, story, specifics, fit, proof. The price arrives second, the justification arrives six times.
4. Build the ladder before adding tiers: an entry piece that is a low-risk gateway, the core that earns the revenue, an aspirational piece that anchors the core, a halo piece that defines the ceiling (Aesop's hand soap to gift kits; Loro Piana's scarf to the $12,000 jacket; Apple's SE to the Vision Pro, as described in the pricing video). Two mistakes: too many tiers before the core is proven; only a core, with no gateway and no anchor.
5. Anchor with something real: a higher tier that exists, a reference price you can defend. A crossed-out price nobody paid is a consumer-protection problem.
6. No perpetual discounts, no timers, no popups, no coupon walls. Permitted: a launch offer with a real end date, a client price for existing clients, a bounded seasonal sale said once.
7. Services state the fee schedule or "from" and list every deliverable under it (labour illusion); bespoke work states the process and the starting point.
8. Trust marks and a short piece of proof sit beside the price; the guarantee, if any, is one plain sentence.

## Premium page patterns by job

Short form; section anatomy and hero mechanics belong to `skills/web-design/references/sections.md`, stage rules to `references/attention-architecture.md`.

- **Luxury brand homepage (job: study, then visit or enquire).** Full-screen stage image or film as the first screen, one line, one quiet action ("Discover", "Book an appointment"); a horizontal nav of four to seven plain nouns; chapters for the maison, the craft, the pieces; no prices on the homepage but prices on every piece; services (delivery, engraving, returns, advisors) listed as facts in the footer and on the product; nothing comparative, nothing discounted.
- **High-ticket product page (job: buy with confidence).** One image owns the viewport; name and rounded price in the first screen; then story, five specifics, fit or dimensions, origin and craft, quiet proof, services as facts; size or spec guide within reach of the selector; curated "worn with" or "pairs with"; a numbered run or seasonal note stated once if true.
- **Premium service or consultation page (job: book or enquire).** The buyer's outcome in the h1; who does the work, photographed; the method as outcomes; what a first conversation includes; fees or "from"; registrations and credentials as facts; a few detailed client accounts; the booking or enquiry form tested first on a phone and ending with what happens next.
- **Attention-grabbing launch page (job: be remembered and join the list).** A curiosity gap in the first screen (one concrete detail plus the date), the one signature moment, real scarcity stated once, the makers named, a single list-join action, a shareable ending; no countdown, no preloader; the memory test run with five real buyers before launch.
- **Premium audit of an existing page.** Run "Audit mode" below; no redesign until the three biggest moves are agreed.

## Attention-grabbing launch pages

A launch page is the one place this skill allows loudness, and it still has to pass the memory and care tests.

1. One hook in the first screen: a curiosity gap made of a concrete detail and the date, or the one signature moment itself; never a countdown or a "coming soon" with nothing to be curious about.
2. One job: join the list, book the first season, pre-order a numbered run. The button says which.
3. Real scarcity, stated once as a fact (the run size, the dates, the number of seats); the booking engine or order system is the only source of counts.
4. The makers named and photographed; the thing shown as it is (renders labelled when it does not exist yet).
5. A shareable ending: the confirmation says what happens next and gives the one line and image a buyer would forward.
6. Memory test before launch with five real buyers; measurement by hook rate, hold rate, list conversion and the two-week recall, not by designer applause.
7. Performance budget unchanged: a launch page that stutters on a weak phone on launch day fails in public.
8. Hand-offs: words and the launch sequence to launch-marketing; the film to launch-video; the direction (Colour-Field or 3D Showcase may be allowed here once) to web-design.

## Evidence honesty box

Do not repeat these in briefs, copy or client decks; the correct versions are in `references/evidence.md` §4.

- "Users decide in 50 ms": the 2006 study measured rated appeal of flashed screenshots; nobody left anything. Say: an appeal judgement forms almost instantly, is stable, and colours later trust.
- "Visuals are processed 60,000x faster than text": no study exists; traced to a 1998 sales deck.
- "92% read reviews / 88% trust them like friends": a 2014 vendor survey of about 2,100 people with conditional answers; no source for the 92%.
- "Zeigarnik: unfinished things stick": a 2025 meta-analysis finds no memory effect; only the tendency to resume holds.
- "Video heroes convert 80% better" and "3D gives +94%": vendor correlations with no method; the same vendors do not recommend autoplay.
- "Offer three options max": the 2010 meta-analysis found a mean effect near zero; overload bites only for uncertain buyers facing complex sets, which is an argument for curation, not for the number three.
- "94% of first impressions are design" and "Stanford says design is 75% of credibility": garbled codings from a 15-person study and a non-representative survey.

## Before hand-off checklist

Confirm each line before web-design starts the draw (or before the build spec goes to a developer in alone mode). Anything unconfirmed is written into the brief as open.

- Tier named from the five answers; the one job and the one button written.
- Personas from data with intent, action and awareness; separate pages decided where personas diverge.
- Position written as identity, world, counterposition; register quiet or loud, with the reason.
- Every planned section mapped to a journey stage, in stage order; sections with no stage deleted.
- Imagery exists or is scheduled: shot list, photographer, dates; renders labelled; AI imagery of product, people, food or places banned in writing.
- Proof inventory complete with sources and permissions; nothing invented; the missing items listed for the client to collect.
- Pricing decided: shown or "from", rounded, the ladder named, the never-do list (discounts, timers, popups) agreed with the client.
- Copy depth set from stakes; the five specifics and the deliverables list drafted or requested; words handed to launch-marketing.
- The signature motion moment named in one sentence; everything else declared quiet.
- Direction shortlist and exclusions passed to web-design; palette commitment and logo scale stated.
- Measurement plan written with dates for the five-second test, VisAWI-S, the two-week memory test and the first benchmark review.
- Refusals recorded with reasons, so the client sees what was left out on purpose.

## Audit mode

Use when the user asks why their premium site looks cheap, does not convert, or wants a conversion audit before a redesign.

1. Record the before: screenshots at 1440, 768 and 390 with motion settled, the fonts and colours in use, load time on a throttled mid-range phone, and the analytics the client has (traffic by source, conversion by page, scroll depth, recordings).
2. Run the stakes test from the client's real price list and buyer risk; most "looks cheap" complaints are a tier mismatch (a luxury price on a normal-tier page), so name the gap.
3. Score the Premium Audit scorecard cell by cell from the screenshots and recordings; quote what you saw, never what the code says.
4. Run the five-second stranger test on the first screen and the headings-only skim on the whole page with someone who has not seen the brief; two wrong answers fail the hero.
5. Check the honesty floor: every proof item traceable, every scarcity claim true, prices shown or "from", no timers, no AI product or people imagery.
6. Report the three changes that would move the page most (usually imagery, first-screen clarity and proof placement), then the full table, then the tier-appropriate plan: a restyle inside the current layout, or a redesign through web-design with this skill's constraints.

## Worked examples

**Aesthetic clinic that wants to charge the top of its city.** Stakes test: price premium, risk high (face, health), ambition "best in the city", budget custom, attention steady; tier premium. Job: book a consultation. Personas from the appointment book: a 35-45 professional researching for months (problem-aware) and a referred patient (product-aware); one page each for the two lead treatments. Position: quiet register; identity "someone who researches and chooses the careful one"; world of consulting rooms and daylight, not spa petals. Imagery: the real doctors, the real rooms, before-and-after only with consent and the same light; no AI faces. Proof: registrations and credentials stated as facts, a few detailed patient accounts, the doctor's published work; no star rows. Copy depth high: method, who performs what, what a consultation includes, risks stated plainly. Pricing "from" per treatment, rounded. Motion: one held picture of the consultation room with passing captions. Direction constraint for web-design: Quiet Editorial or Soft and Calm with a colour from the actual rooms. Refused: countdown offers, "limited slots this month", stock models, a floating "chat now" bubble.

**Wealth-management firm for business owners aged 50+.** Tier luxury (life savings, a once-in-a-life sale). Job: request a private conversation. Awareness: most-aware, referred, sceptical of flash. Memory test result from the firm's own survey of its customers: the conventional designs win; difference goes into voice (plain, exact, unhurried), the founders photographed in their own office, and a single signature moment (the firm's real 20-year track record drawn as one quiet line). Copy depth: deep; a three-word hero would read as evasive; above the fold the method, the people and the regulator registration. Proof: audited figures with periods, named client letters with permission, press; no "trusted by 10,000 families". Pricing: fee schedule published as a table, rounded. Direction constraint: Corporate Trust or Quiet Editorial; Glass / Spatial and 3D excluded. Refused: liquid-glass panels, 3D coins, autoplay video, a chatbot in the hero, any urgency.

**High-ticket D2C apparel product page (Rs 18,000 jacket).** Tier premium. Job: add to bag with confidence about fit. Value sequence: image, name, price, story, specifics, fit, proof; the price is justified six times before the button. Imagery: the five shots (editorial in a real street, macro of the weave close enough to count, movement, hardware, ghost silhouette), same grading as the rest of the store, one image owning the viewport. Specifics: fabric weight, mill, construction, fit intent, behaviour after washing. Fit signals: model height and size on the photo, fit-intent copy, measurements per size, customer photos across bodies. Proof: brand-coloured ratings, three detailed reviews on durability, stockists and press. Pricing: rounded, no "was", a numbered seasonal run stated once. Motion: a shutter image on load, nothing else. Refused: countdown, "12 people viewing", spin-to-win, a bundle builder in the viewport, AI model imagery.

**Industrial B2B supplier with forty years of history.** Tier premium (buyers are engineers risking a plant shutdown). Job: request a quote. The homepage stops being a wall of part numbers: lead with the track record and the applications, put the catalog behind a standardised structure (specs, applications, quote route) built for engineers, show the real plant and the real people. Proof: certifications as facts, named installations with permission, downtime figures with periods. Copy: specificity is the whole argument; grade-level readability still applies. Direction constraint: Swiss Industrial or Corporate Trust. Motion: rolling figures for three real numbers. Refused: a "we are the leader" hero metric, stock handshakes, a "founded in 1986" ego section (the forty years appear as what they bought the buyer, not as a milestone).

**Boutique hotel launch page (eight rooms, opening in spring).** Tier luxury; attention goal high. Job: join the opening list and book the first season. Position: loud in imagery, quiet in words; the world is the building, the valley and the food. Hook: a curiosity gap in the hero (one detail of the restored building, the opening date, a single line), a slow crossfade of three real photographs taken on site now, not renders, or a 3D Showcase of the building if renders are all that exist, labelled as renders. Real scarcity stated once: eight rooms, first season dates. Proof: the architects and the chef named, press on the restoration. Pricing: nightly "from" per room category, rounded. Motion signature: an opening window from one window of the building to the whole facade. Direction constraint: Luxury Monochrome, Dark Cinematic or 3D Showcase. Refused: a countdown to opening (a date in the headline instead), "only 3 rooms left" unless the booking engine says so, stock food photography, a preloader.

## Anti-patterns and refusals

- Fake scarcity: countdown timers on evergreen offers, "X people viewing", random low-stock bars, spin-to-win. Refuse; they lower perceived benevolence and regulators treat them as deceptive. Offer real scarcity stated once.
- AI imagery of the product, people, food or places; stock handshakes, boardroom high-fives, a smiling stranger as the team. Refuse; propose a phone shoot today and a proper shoot in the plan.
- Discount blasts, "was/now" strike-throughs, charm endings and coupon walls on a premium page. Refuse; price plainly and build the ladder.
- Flashy motion for its own sake: preloaders, sideways scroll as the thesis, 3D that exists to impress designers, kinetic type everywhere. Refuse beyond the one signature moment.
- "Award-style" designs that fail the memory test for the actual buyer; liquid glass and 3D for an audience that said they would not open it.
- Hidden pricing on high-ticket where "from" would qualify the buyer; it reads as expensive and wastes calls.
- Fabricated proof of any kind, including "trusted by 10,000", invented press, borrowed logos, placeholder reviews with real-sounding names.
- Five calls to action on one page; a hero that sells, recruits and raises at once.
- Corporate ego: founder letters, milestone timelines, "a message from the chairman" ahead of what the buyer came for.
- Minimalism as evasion: a three-word page for a buyer risking their savings or health.
- Over-reduction into a template with good manners: restraint without bespoke substance reads as laziness, not curation.

## Special cases

- **Existing luxury brand with guidelines.** Keep the brand's faces, marks and photography rules; this skill audits the page against the journey stages and the honesty floor only, and names any guideline that is pulling the page toward the cheap column (a loud logo lockup, a mandatory promo bar).
- **Pre-launch product with renders only.** Renders are allowed when the thing does not exist yet; label them, keep one grading, and plan the real shoot as the first post-launch task. Never present a render as a photograph.
- **Premium brand, mass-market channel (marketplace listing, aggregator).** The page cannot control the skeleton; put the whole budget into imagery, specifics and quiet proof, and keep the price rounded.
- **Multi-audience site (clients and candidates, patients and referring doctors).** Split the first screen into two clear pathways or give each audience its own landing page; never ask one hero to serve both.
- **Premium clinic or firm in India.** The stakes test uses local price bands; forms that collect names, phones or health data follow `dpdp-legal-compliance`; a WhatsApp action is normal and still premium when it is the only secondary action.
- **Redesign of a page that already converts.** Audit first; change imagery, first-screen clarity and proof placement before touching the layout; prove each change against the before.

## When the user pushes back

- "Add a countdown, it converts": it converts for consumables; on a premium page the only controlled study found lower trust, and fake timers are a named deceptive pattern. Offer the real scarcity line and the two-week test (turn timers off, watch average order value).
- "We cannot afford a photographer": a phone photograph of the real shop, team or product beats a perfect fake; plan the proper shoot for phase two and list the stand-ins to replace.
- "Make it ultra-minimal like Apple": Apple's pages are long and specific below a quiet hero; show the stakes test and propose a quiet first screen with a thorough page.
- "Hide the price, it is bespoke": publish "from" and what it includes; the buyers who were never going to pay stop calling, and the ones who can trust you more.
- "Put the testimonials on their own page": proof works where hesitation peaks, beside the action and the price; keep a wall of love as a secondary page if wanted.
- "Our competitor uses glass and 3D": run the memory test with five of the client's real buyers before copying anything; keep the skeleton familiar and spend difference on voice and imagery.
- "More sections, we have a lot to say": cut by half twice, then add back only what the stakes require; depth is measured in specifics, not sections.
